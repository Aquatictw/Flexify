# Core doctrine

## The operating principles

5/3/1 is organized around five ideas:

1. **Start lighter than you think you need to.** Build technical quality, bar speed, work capacity, and tolerance before chasing load.
2. **Progress slowly.** Training is accumulated practice, not a weekly max test. Add no more than 5 lb to the press and bench press TM and 10 lb to the squat and deadlift TM after a successful cycle; smaller increases are allowed.
3. **Center training on large, efficient movements.** The primary lifts are squat, deadlift, bench press, and press. Apply the same economy elsewhere: basic jumps and medicine-ball throws for power, and simple running, sled, or Prowler work for conditioning.
4. **Pursue many kinds of personal record.** A PR can be another rep, cleaner technique, better speed, a small load increase, or a conditioning milestone. Set a goal before a PR set, stop when form materially deteriorates, and normally keep one or two good reps in reserve. Lower the weight under control and drive it up as fast as sound technique permits.
5. **Keep the program in balance.** Strength, conditioning, mobility, and recovery support one another. Balance does not mean equal effort in every area; it means no area is neglected enough to limit the others.

Do not mistake a hard day for proof that the plan has failed. Over a long training career, ordinary and poor sessions are inevitable. Evaluate trends across cycles rather than reacting to a single workout.

## Training max: selection, testing, and correction

The training max (TM) is a programming tool, not a true max and not a status symbol. Main and supplemental percentages are based on it.

An estimated 1RM can be calculated as:

`estimated 1RM = weight × reps × 0.0333 + weight`

A TM is generally 85–90% of the resulting actual or estimated max. Higher-volume programs often call for an 85% TM, and some trainees do better beginning around 80–85%. Stronger or more advanced lifters may need an even more conservative TM. Follow the requirement of the chosen template rather than forcing one TM percentage onto every lift.

The correct TM permits fast, controlled work even on an average day. Different lifts may eventually require different degrees of conservatism.

### TM test

Use the 7th Week TM Test to verify maxes after completing the programmed Leader and Anchor phases and before beginning a new Leader. Between the Leader and Anchor themselves, use the 7th Week Deload. Operationally, begin new programming with either a TM Test or the less common PR Test:

- 70% TM × 5
- 80% TM × 5
- 90% TM × 5
- 100% TM × 3–5

For a target 90% TM, the lifter should perform at least 3 strong, fast reps with the TM. For a target 85% TM, the target is 5 strong, fast reps. This is not intended to become a true 3RM or 5RM, and the final set need not go to failure.

If the lifter makes more than 5 reps, retain the normal cycle increase; do not accelerate the TM. If only 1–2 reps are possible, lower the TM. Recalculate the estimated max from that performance and choose the template-appropriate percentage. A conservative coaching standard is to lower the TM whenever 5 strong reps are not available, particularly for a program built around an 85% TM.

Resetting a TM is normal, especially after years of training and especially on the press and bench press. Do not assume all four lifts must progress at the same rate.

## Main, supplemental, and assistance work

The four main lifts remain squat, deadlift, bench press, and press. If injury prevents one, substitute a movement from the same family, such as an incline press or floor press for a press. Do not replace a main lift with an unrelated isolation movement.

The classic three-week main-work progression is:

- Week 1: 65% × 5, 75% × 5, 85% × 5+
- Week 2: 70% × 3, 80% × 3, 90% × 3+
- Week 3: 75% × 5, 85% × 3, 95% × 1+

A supplemental lift is barbell work that repeats the main lift or uses a close variation. Not every template includes supplemental work. If a different lift is used, establish a separate, honest TM for it.

Common supplemental choices:

- **Deadlift:** deficit deadlift, rack pull, block pull, snatch-grip deadlift, fat-bar deadlift, double-overhand deadlift, or trap-bar deadlift.
- **Squat:** front squat, box squat (including specialty-bar variants), safety-squat-bar squat, or Manta Ray squat.
- **Bench/press:** a different bench grip; incline or floor press; or bench, incline, floor, and overhead pressing with a football bar or log.

BBB, First Set Last, Second Set Last, and Boring But Strong are examples of supplemental structures. Use a variation because it serves the program, not because it looks like a clever fix for a supposed weak muscle. Before specializing, establish basic whole-body strength, relative strength, conditioning, consistency, and sound execution.

Assistance is the third layer. It develops useful muscle, balances repeated barbell patterns, and supplies work that the main and supplemental lifts do not. It must remain recoverable. If assistance compromises the main work or the rest of the program, reduce its difficulty or volume.

## Planning Leaders, Anchors, and the 7th Week

Plan several cycles rather than improvising one workout at a time. Newer trainees can usually plan farther ahead; experienced lifters should use shorter planning horizons.

Common Leader/Anchor sequences are:

- **3 Leaders / 2 Anchors:** mainly for beginners and BBB or BBS challenges; not the general recommendation.
- **2 Leaders / 2 Anchors:** suitable for beginners and intermediates.
- **2 Leaders / 1 Anchor:** suitable across experience levels and the default recommendation when the templates allow it.

Two Leader cycles give enough exposure to adapt and judge whether the work helps each lift. The Anchor then reveals progress without extending demanding Leader volume until it causes burnout.

### Leader

A Leader generally uses:

- more barbell volume, usually through supplemental work;
- less assistance;
- fewer jumps and throws;
- less hard conditioning;
- an emphasis on easy conditioning.

### Anchor

An Anchor generally uses:

- less total barbell volume;
- higher relative intensity or harder-pushed main work;
- more assistance;
- more jumps and throws;
- more hard conditioning, while easy conditioning may continue.

Mobility normally remains steady in both phases. These are guidelines, not a claim that every named template follows the same pattern. Choose a plan the trainee can recover from, not merely one that looks impressive.

### 7th Week Protocol

“7th Week” names the protocol; it is not necessarily performed every seventh calendar week. It can serve as a deload, TM test, or occasional PR test.

Use a deload between every Leader and Anchor. Use a TM Test or PR Test before new programming. A lifter may also deload after any cycle when age, accumulated fatigue, or a demanding template warrants it.

All versions reduce total volume, omit supplemental work, and limit assistance.

**Deload**

- 70% × 5
- 80% × 3–5
- 90% × 1
- 100% TM × 1

Use this when the TM is known to be correct and fatigue needs to be managed. A deload should be scheduled before exhaustion makes normal training impossible.

**PR Test**

- 70% × 5
- 80% × 5
- 90% × 5
- 100% TM × a planned rep goal or PR

This is useful after a phase dominated by 5s Progression or when an athlete must keep most regular training lighter but still benefits from a controlled competitive test. If the final set cannot produce 3–5 reps, correct the TM.

The protocol can be arranged over 2, 3, or 4 training days. Perform 10 total jumps or throws before the lift or lifts of each day. Assistance is 25–50 total reps in each category—push, pull, and single-leg/core—and may be reduced further or changed to joint-friendly movements. Do 3–5 easy conditioning sessions. Hard conditioning is normally removed, although a deliberately scheduled conditioning test can replace it; place lifting around that test intelligently.

## Warm-up and movement preparation

Consistency matters more than novelty. For most trainees, 10–15 minutes before lifting is sufficient once the habit is established. Older, more experienced, stiff, or previously inconsistent lifters may need more. Warm up before every hard lifting or conditioning session.

A useful general sequence is:

1. Move easily for 3–5 minutes with an AirDyne, jump rope, light sled, or Prowler; do not turn this into conditioning.
2. Open the groin and hips laterally with hurdle duck-unders, Cossack squats, side shuffles, or side lunges.
3. Add a bodyweight movement such as a push-up, chin-up, ab-wheel rollout, or inchworm.
4. Use a leg/hip-flexor movement such as a paused Bulgarian split squat, lunge, or Spiderman.
5. Prepare the shoulders with band pull-aparts or dislocates.
6. Settle into a deep bodyweight squat for about 30 seconds.

Move in several directions, include getting down to and up from the floor, and favor slow, controlled, full-range reps. Quality is more important than accumulating warm-up volume.

The Agile 8 is another complete option:

- IT-band foam roll: 10–15 passes per leg.
- Adductor foam roll: 10–15 passes per leg.
- Glute/piriformis release: 30 seconds per side.
- Rollovers into V-sits: 10 reps.
- Fire-hydrant circles: 10 forward and 10 backward per leg.
- Mountain climbers: 10 reps.
- Groiners: 10 reps, holding the last for 10 seconds.
- Hip-flexor stretch: 3 × 10 seconds per leg.

## Jumps and medicine-ball throws

Use an explosive movement after the general warm-up and before lifting to prepare the whole body to produce force quickly. The objective is consistent, high-quality exposure—not fatigue, a daily max, or a conditioning circuit.

General prescription:

- 10–20 total jumps or throws per training session, adjusted to the program;
- full rest between efforts;
- explosive execution on every rep;
- for most trained men, a 10–12 lb medicine ball is sufficient; weaker athletes should use a lighter ball;
- never trade speed for load.

Do not use high-rep box jumps. Choose a movement appropriate to the trainee’s skill and tissues. Ordinary jumps and throws are broadly useful; reactive plyometrics, which require an immediate rebound after landing, are much more stressful and are not necessary for most people.

### Jump options

- **Box jump:** Jump with the arms and land softly. Choose a box that requires intent but can be made reliably; about 80–90% of the lifter’s best or estimated best height is a useful range. When box jumps are the only jump, perform 3–5 sets of 3–5, using lower boxes in early sets.
- **Standing long jump:** Jump from two feet for distance and stick a balanced two-foot landing. When used alone, perform 5 × 3. Landing impact makes it somewhat more stressful than a box jump.
- **Standing hurdle jump:** Jump over the hurdle and land under control. Beginners should start with 3 × 3; after adapting, the box-jump volume can be used.
- **Standing triple jump:** Begin with a two-foot jump, then two bounds. Keep ground contact short, stay on the balls of the feet during the bounds, use the arms, and stick only the final landing. Start with short, controlled efforts. This is optional and more stressful to ankles and knees.
- **Bounding box jumps:** Step from one box, rebound from the balls of the feet, and jump to the next. Start with a low drop box and a higher landing box. A multi-box version alternates sticking each box landing with stepping off and rebounding to the next. Limit this to 15 contacts per session.
- **Bounding hurdle jumps:** Rebound over a row of hurdles with minimal ground contact. This is advanced, stressful work for athletes with prepared ankles, knees, rhythm, and prior bounding experience; most lifters do not need it.

### Throw options

- **Standing overhead throw:** From a parallel or staggered stance, extend the whole body and throw forward from overhead; the feet should leave the floor.
- **Standing backward throw:** Face away from the target, load the hips with a wide stance, then project the ball overhead for height and distance while keeping the trunk braced.
- **Standing chest pass:** Drive through the whole body rather than turning it into an arm exercise; a small forward jump is normal.

Mix the three throws for 15–20 total throws, resting enough to preserve intent.

### Pairing explosive work with lifting

After a trainee has adapted to regular jumping, throwing, and lifting, a jump or throw can be paired with main-lift warm-up sets and, at most, the first one or two work sets. Use no more than 3 explosive reps between barbell sets. Each effort still needs full attention and recovery. A lower-body jump can prime pressing, and a medicine-ball throw can accompany lower-body work; these are whole-body power drills, not muscle-isolation pairings.

# Assistance work

Each normal training day includes three categories:

- **Push**
- **Pull**
- **Single-leg or core**

The broad base recommendation is 50–100 total reps in each category per workout, but the active template’s specific limits take precedence. Usually choose one or two exercises in each category. The category total may be split—for example, a smaller amount of weighted pulling plus higher-rep face pulls.

Assistance should balance the program and remain recoverable:

- With high squat/deadlift or supplemental volume, avoid piling on heavy rows and lower-back work; choose face pulls, band pull-aparts, and easier trunk work.
- Harder assistance is more reasonable when barbell volume is lower.
- Bodyweight movements often add joint-friendly, recoverable volume, but still require full range and controlled reps. Bodyweight work is limited on its own and should not be a weaker lifter’s only assistance; retain appropriate dumbbell, kettlebell, band, or cable work as well.
- Change exercises between sessions if useful; the work and category balance matter more than maintaining a fixed novelty-free list.
- Before diagnosing a sophisticated “weak point,” develop basic capacity in chins, push-ups, dips, hanging leg raises, and back raises.

## Push catalog

- **Dips:** Build triceps, shoulders, chest, upper-body size, and pressing support. Favor sets of at least 8 rather than heavy triples. In a 100-rep day, up to 50 may be weighted before finishing with bodyweight reps. Use dips no more than once or twice per week, and replace them if shoulder pain interferes with the main lifts.
- **Push-ups:** A joint-friendly dip alternative and a way to practice whole-body tension for pressing. Add a vest, chains, or a plate when needed. Keep the shoulder blades controlled and the torso rigid; occasional 3–5-second pauses at the bottom teach position.
- **Dumbbell bench, incline, or standing press:** Adds pressing volume with a free implement. Preserve full range rather than shortening the movement to handle more weight.
- **Triceps extensions or pushdowns:** Direct triceps work with cables, bars, ropes, or bands. Choose a comfortable attachment. Keep extensions strict enough that they do not become presses, and avoid versions that irritate the elbows.
- **Full-range plate raise:** Trains shoulders and upper back without more heavy pressing. Raise a plate or football bar with straight arms to overhead and finish with the upper back engaged. Use higher reps.

## Pull catalog

- **Chin-ups and pull-ups:** Primary vertical pulling. Use varied grips, bars, ropes, or towels; avoid leg and torso momentum. Unlike dips, lower-rep weighted work can be appropriate. Assistance or partial unloading is useful while building capacity.
- **Inverted rows:** A scalable row for trainees who cannot yet chin and useful high-rep work for those who can. Keep the body rigid; raise the bar or straps when form sags. A vest can increase difficulty.
- **Rows:** Dumbbell rows are the preferred general option because they train the upper back, lats, grip, and deadlift lockout with less lower-back cost than barbell rows. T-bar, barbell, and machine rows are alternatives. Treat rows as muscular assistance, not a max-effort main lift. For dumbbell rows, high-rep work in the 20–50 range with the heaviest load that preserves reasonable form is especially useful.
- **Curls:** Provide elbow-flexor and arm work to balance frequent pressing and may support shoulder health. Barbell, dumbbell, cable, and machine versions are all acceptable.
- **Lat pulldown:** A valid machine-based vertical pull, particularly when chins are unavailable or inappropriate, though chins, rows, and inverted rows are generally preferred.
- **Band pull-aparts:** Low-cost upper-back and shoulder work suitable for training days or daily use. Use a full range and briefly hold the arms-out position; light bands and varied angles are fine.
- **Face pulls:** A somewhat more demanding pull-apart alternative using a band, cable, or sled. Row toward the face, keep the load modest, and accumulate clean higher reps rather than chasing a max.
- **Rear lateral raises:** Upper-back/rear-shoulder work with plates, dumbbells, or cables. Use strict form and higher reps.
- **Upright rows:** Optional trap work with a barbell or low cable. They suit some shoulders and irritate others; omit them when uncomfortable. Their transfer beyond trap development is limited.
- **Shrugs:** Optional trap work with a barbell, trap bar, dumbbells, or kettlebells. Use an actual controlled shoulder elevation rather than merely moving the head.

## Single-leg and core catalog

- **Back raises and reverse hyperextensions:** High-rep posterior-chain work for the lower back, glutes, and hamstrings that complements heavier low-rep squats and pulls. Focus the target tissues and do not let it become momentum-only work. A 45-degree back raise is a practical option.
- **Glute-ham raise:** Trains the lower back, glutes, and hamstrings without loading a bar in the hands or on the back. A Nordic variation works without a machine but may be uncomfortable or stressful around the knees.
- **Straight-leg deadlift and good morning:** Direct posterior-chain assistance. Use lighter loads and honest range; these are not main or supplemental lifts. Excess soreness signals too much load or volume.
- **Abdominal work:** Direct trunk work remains valuable. Favor longer sets or higher reps. Ab-wheel rollouts and hanging leg raises are strong defaults; straps can spare painful shoulders, and a vest can load rollouts. A practical baseline is the ability to perform 10 slow hanging leg raises.
- **Single-leg work:** Step-ups, lunges, and Bulgarian split squats can be loaded with a barbell, dumbbells, kettlebells, or a vest. Choose by tolerance and access. Preserve stride, depth, and single-leg contribution instead of increasing weight until the movement becomes abbreviated or bilateral.
- **Kettlebell swings, cleans, and snatches:** Useful assistance with one or two bells; a T-handle can substitute for swings. They supplement rather than replace barbell strength work.
- **Farmer walk:** Builds back, grip, and trunk strength. Beginners and inexperienced lifters should not use heavy carries as conditioning while fatigued. An advanced lifter may use them for strength and conditioning. A general workout target is 240–400 total yards with a manageable load.
- **Goblet squat:** A simple squat assistance option, especially for beginners and within Krypteia.

## Neck work

Neck work sits outside the three assistance categories. Use a 4-way neck machine when available; accessible alternatives are neck-harness extensions and bench-supported neck flexion. Work through a full range. Begin with low volume and higher reps; increase slowly because excessive early fatigue can be unusually uncomfortable. It is optional for most trainees but useful for contact athletes. A broad target is 50–100 total reps per workout once adapted.

## Bodyweight standards and tests

Basic reference abilities include 10 chin-ups or pull-ups, 25 push-ups, 15 dips, 10 slow hanging leg raises, and 20 back raises.

For a more formal test, the book gives the following standards for men. Perform as many clean reps as possible in 10 minutes:

| Movement | 10-minute standard |
| --- | ---: |
| Push-ups | 100 |
| Dips | 75 |
| Chin-ups/pull-ups | 50 |
| Hanging leg raises | 50 |

Test only one movement in a workout, place it away from conflicting heavy work, and retest every few weeks rather than every session. Advanced trainees can repeat push-up, dip, chin-up, or pull-up tests with a vest loaded to 10% or 20% of bodyweight; the long-term target is to reach the same standard with 20% added.

# Template reference

## Beginner Prep School

**Purpose and trainee.** A three-day rotating A/B foundation for true beginners,
young athletes, and adults who need basic training competence. It develops lift
technique, relative strength, jumping/landing, work capacity, and the ability to
run. Do not rush out of it: the exit standard is competence and conditioning,
not a particular barbell number.

**TM and progression.** After technique is stable, work up in sets of five,
stopping at slowed bar speed or obvious technical breakdown rather than a miss.
Estimate 1RM as `weight × reps × .0333 + weight`; use a 90% TM for the relatively
strong/technically sound lifts and 85% for weak or technically poor lifts. Raise
TMs after a cycle, but weak beginners may add only 5 lb even to squat/deadlift,
repeat the TM, or use fractional plates. There is no fixed duration.

**Schedule.** Alternate:

- Workout A: squat and bench press.
- Workout B: deadlift and press.

The three-week main work is all sets of five:

| Week | Main work | Supplemental |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 70%×5, 80%×5, 90%×5 | 5×5 at 70% FSL |
| 2 | 65%×5, 75%×5, 85%×5 | 5×5 at 65% FSL |
| 3 | 75%×5, 85%×5, 95%×5 | 5×5 at 75% FSL |

For a lift assigned an 85% TM because it is weak or technically poor, SSL may
replace FSL, still for 5×5. Every rep should be crisp. Superset low-cost
bodyweight work: push-ups between squat main sets; sit-ups, chins/inverted rows,
face pulls, or band pull-aparts during supplemental work. Do not take these to
failure.

**Prep and assistance.**

- Warm-up circuit, 3 rounds: jumping jacks 25, bodyweight squats 10, mountain
  climbers 10/leg.
- Box jump or standing long jump: 10–20 quality landings per workout; no depth
  jumps.
- Four-movement assistance circuit, generally 3–5 rounds: KB swing/snatch or
  dumbbell/bodyweight squat 25–100 total; push-ups/dips 25–100; chins/pull-ups
  25–50; ab wheel/hanging leg raise 25–50. Use inverted rows if chins are not
  possible. Start below the target totals when needed.
- Example circuit A, 5 rounds: DB squat 20, push-ups 10, chins 5, ab wheel 10.
- Example circuit B, 5 rounds: KB swing 15, dips 10, Fat Man/inverted rows 15,
  hanging leg raise 10.

Main plus supplemental work for each lift should eventually take 15–18 minutes
(30–36 minutes for both lifts), without rushing technique. Assistance should fit
in 20 minutes.

**Running and graduation goals.** Run three times/week, 1–3 miles, with the basic
goal of being able to run a mile. A track alternative is one of: 10–16×100 m,
6–8×200 m, 4–6×400 m, or 2–3×800 m; run rather than sprint, with unprescribed
rest. Bodyweight assistance can also be done on non-lifting days. Graduate only
when the lifter can meet the lifting and circuit time standards, run a mile, jump
and land correctly, and demonstrate a balanced base.

**If a lift stalls.** Back up three cycles and either repeat; repeat while adding
a controlled PR/goal set; raise supplemental volume to 7–10×5 FSL; use 5×5/3/1;
or use SSL. Also audit food and sleep.

## Boring But Big (BBB)

**Role and purpose.** Always a Leader. It is primarily a size template that can
also build strength in younger/intermediate lifters. It is not appropriate for
true/very new beginners, athletes whose sport work must dominate, or most
advanced lifters. The printed summary nevertheless specifies 85% TM for most
lifters and 90% for “beginners”; treat that as a narrow authorial exception, not
a recommendation for unskilled novices. Eat enough to support the volume.

**Schedule.** Three or four days/week. Four-day is one main lift plus the same
lift's 5×10; three-day rotates the four lift days so each workout repeats every
ninth day. Forever BBB should use the same lift for main and supplemental work.
Original BBB may pair opposite lifts, such as bench main work and press 5×10.

### Forever BBB

Use 5's PRO main work. The printed example uses 3/5/1; ordinary 5/3/1 is also
allowed.

| Week | Main work | Beginner/intermediate BBB | Advanced intermediate, recovery-limited, or new to BBB |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 70%×5, 80%×5, 90%×5 | 5×10 at 60% | 5×10 at 50% |
| 2 | 65%×5, 75%×5, 85%×5 | 5×10 at 50% | 5×10 at 40% |
| 3 | 75%×5, 85%×5, 95%×5 | 5×10 at 70% | 5×10 at 60% |

“Forever BBB Light” is the same prescription with 3×10 rather than 5×10.

### Original BBB

Use 5's PRO main work and 5×10 at a constant 40–60% across all three weeks.
Start around 50–55%; use a lift-specific percentage and start lighter than
expected. The squat and especially deadlift often need lower percentages.

### Full Body BBB

For experienced lifters with excellent recovery; begin all BBB work at 50% and
adjust by bar speed. Four-day example:

- Monday: deadlift 5's PRO; bench 5×10.
- Tuesday: squat 5×10; press 5's PRO.
- Thursday: squat 5's PRO; press 5×10.
- Friday: deadlift 5×10; bench 5's PRO.

The three-day version rotates these pairings every ninth day. A useful deadlift
variant performs BBB with double-overhand grip and bases its percentage on a
separate estimated double-overhand deadlift max, reducing low-back strain while
training grip.

### BBB Challenge

Only after at least three preparatory BBB cycles. Start with a correct TM:
90% is the absolute maximum; 85% is better for lifters with several years of
proper training. Use 5's PRO (classic 5/3/1 or 3/5/1), no PR sets. Across three
cycles:

- Cycle 1: 5×10 at 50%.
- Cycle 2: 5×10 at 60%.
- Cycle 3: 5×10 at 70%.

Raise the TM normally between cycles. Eat enough, follow the program exactly,
and do not use this as a post-layoff “jump start.”

### BBB at FSL

For long-range programming only, with at most an 85% TM and often a TM selected
from the weight the lifter can truly sustain for the 5×10. Standard 5/3/1 main
work followed by 5×10 at FSL: 65%, 70%, 75%. 3/5/1 may also be used. The book
frames this as a 10–12-cycle planning decision, not an experiment to play by ear.

### Slightly Less BBB

Options:

- 3×10 at a constant 40–60% after 5's PRO.
- “Slightly Less Forever BBB”: 3×10 at 60%, 50%, 70%.
- Use BBB for only two lifts (one press and squat or deadlift); use 5×5 FSL on
  the other two. This is particularly suitable for older/recovery-limited
  lifters.
- Perform squat BBB on both squat and deadlift days, and optionally bench BBB on
  both press days; do **not** duplicate deadlift BBB.
- Short-session version: after 5's PRO, perform one set of 10 each at 50%, 60%,
  and 70% (or descend 70/60/50).

**Jumps, assistance, conditioning.** Ten low-stress jumps/throws per workout.
Push 25–50, pull 25–50, single-leg/core 0–50. Prefer abs over lower-back and
single-leg work, and low-stress push assistance such as pushdowns/extensions
over weighted dips. Hard conditioning: at most twice/week, on lifting days and
preferably squat/deadlift days or before a rest day. Easy conditioning: 3–5
days. BBB's goal is size/strength, not peak conditioning.

**Anchor choices after BBB.** PR set + FSL; PR set + Jokers + FSL; 5's
Progression + Jokers + FSL; 5/3/1 + Widowmakers; 5's Progression + FSL; Full
Body 5's PRO; Full Body PR Set; Beyond FSL; or Original 5/3/1.

## First Set Last (FSL) family

**Role and use.** FSL can be Leader or Anchor, works at every level, and adds
sustainable volume without crushing bar speed. Use an 85–90% TM. The default is
5×5 at the first work-set percentage: 65%, 70%, 75% in 5/3/1 order, or 70%, 65%,
75% in 3/5/1 order. A Widowmaker alternative is one set of 15–20 (20 goal) at
FSL. Three- or four-day schedules may use same-lift or opposite-lift
supplemental work; the three-day rotation repeats a lift every ninth day.

### 5's PRO + 5×5 FSL

Low-intensity, submaximal base work for athletes, lifters pushing conditioning,
poor recoverers, advanced lifters backing away from peak work, or anyone under
high non-training stress. Main work is 5's PRO; supplemental is 5×5 FSL.

- Base assistance: push, pull, single-leg/core each 50–100.
- If emphasizing assistance: each 100+; remove hard conditioning and use only
  aerobic work.
- If emphasizing conditioning: each 25–50.
- Base conditioning: 2–3 hard days and as much easy work as useful. With four
  hard days, reduce assistance. If bar speed degrades for more than roughly two
  weeks, change schedule, deload timing, recovery, conditioning efficiency, or
  volume; 3×5 FSL is an allowed reduction.

As a Leader it can precede any of the more intensive FSL anchors. As an Anchor it
is useful when PR sets cannot be performed frequently.

### 5's Progression + Jokers + FSL

Use 3/5/1. Week 1: 70/80/90×5, 1–2 Joker sets of 1–3 reps, then 5×5 at 70%.
Week 2: 65/75/85×5, no Jokers, then 5×5 at 65%. Week 3:
75/85/95×5, 1–2 Jokers, then 5×5 at 75%. Jokers rise 5–10% over the day's top
set and are never missed.

### PR Set + FSL

Best as a 3/5/1 Anchor: PR sets only in weeks 1 and 3, followed by 5×5 FSL; week
2 is 5's PRO and 5×5 FSL. Week 1 top set is 90%×3+, week 3 is 95%×1+. The
work-up may use either standard reps or 5's Progression. End a PR set when the
planned record/goal is reached; it is not indiscriminate AMRAP.

### PR Set + Jokers + FSL

Same 3/5/1 structure, adding a few 1–3-rep Jokers after the PR set when bar speed
and readiness justify them. Conditioning sufficient to recover between the PR
and Jokers is a prerequisite; no misses.

### 5/3/1 + Widowmakers

Standard 5/3/1 PR set, then 1×15–20 at FSL: 65%, 70%, 75%. 5's Progression may
precede the PR set. Push squat and pressing Widowmakers; stop deadlift
Widowmakers at form breakdown. This is demanding and unsuitable for poor
technique or weak trunk control.

Layouts include: a hard three-day alternating full-body schedule; a reduced
three-day schedule with hard Widowmakers Monday/Friday and 5×5 FSL squat plus
one press Wednesday; a four-day full-body split that separates squat/deadlift PR
and Widowmaker exposures; or ordinary one-lift-per-day. Use only cyclically,
not year-round.

### Full Body, 5's PRO

Four days/week. Monday squat + bench 5's PRO; Tuesday deadlift + press 5's PRO;
Thursday squat + bench 5×5 FSL; Friday deadlift + press 5×5 FSL. This is
deliberately submaximal: heavier main days early, volume days late.

### Full Body, PR Set

Same four-day layout, but Monday/Tuesday include 5's PRO plus a PR set for both
lifts; Thursday/Friday are 5×5 FSL. Standard work-set reps may replace 5's PRO;
Jokers may follow PRs or replace the PR.

### Beyond + FSL

Experienced lifters only. Work in 10% jumps to the TM for sets of 1–5, then
either stop after 1–5 at TM, take a PR, or use restrained Jokers; follow with
5×5 FSL. Standard four-day is one Beyond lift plus its FSL each day. Full-body
variants alternate Beyond exposures and FSL exposures. In the most intensive
variant, push only one of squat/deadlift and one of bench/press; take the other
pair only to TM for 1–3. Do not run frequent Jokers.

**General FSL assistance and conditioning.** Push, pull, and single-leg/core
50–100 each, reduced as recovery demands. Hard conditioning 2–3 days; easy
conditioning 3–5. A useful mixed plan pushes squat and press with PR/Jokers/FSL
while keeping bench and deadlift on 5's Progression/FSL.

## Full Body, 1000% Awesome

**Leader.** Three days/week for intermediates who want conditioning plus
submaximal volume and cannot lift four days. Not ideal for advanced lifters.
Use an 85% TM for 2–3 cycles.

- Monday: squat 5's PRO; bench 5×5 at 80–85%.
- Wednesday: deadlift 5's PRO; press 5×5 at 80–85%.
- Friday: squat 5×5 at 80–85%; bench 5's PRO.

Press and bench may swap. Use 5/3/1 or 3/5/1. Select 80 versus 85% separately by
bar speed; no grinding. Advanced volume option is 7–8 sets, but return to five if
speed or other lifts suffer. Assistance: each category 25–50. Conditioning:
three hard and three easy days (all-easy is allowed).

**Anchor.** Keep the three-day skeleton but use 5×5 FSL for supplemental work.
Main work may be: PR set + optional Jokers; 5's Progression only; a lift-specific
mix of those; or Beyond. Assistance rises to 50–100 each. Conditioning remains
three hard and three easy days.

## SVR II

**Leader.** Variety-focused size/strength template for experienced,
technically sound lifters who enjoy hard work; not for recent beginners. Use an
85% TM and 3/5/1:

- Week 1: 70/80/90×5 with PR at 90%, then 1×15–20 at 70% (Widowmaker).
- Week 2: 65/75/85×5, then either BBB 5×10 at 65% or BBS 10×5 at 65%.
- Week 3: 75/85/95×5, then 5×5 at 85% (SSL).

It may be applied to only selected lifts; use 5's Progression + 3–5×5 FSL for
the others. Three- or four-day schedules work. Upper-body Widowmakers may use a
rest-pause approach. Assistance: 25–50 each, reducing push or low-back work on
BBB/BBS days. Conditioning: at most two hard, plus 3–5 easy days.

**Anchor, option 1.** Week 1: 70%×5, 80%×5, 90%×5+, then 1×15–20 at 70%.
Week 2: 65%×5, 75%×5, then **5×5 at 85%** (5×5/3/1). Week 3:
75%×5, 85%×5, 95%×5, then 1–2 Jokers.

**Anchor, option 2.** Week 1 is the same. Week 2:
65%×5, 75%×5, 85%×5, then 5×5 at 75% SSL. Week 3 is the same Joker week.
Standard reps or 5's Progression may be used. Two lifts may use SVR II while the
others remain lighter; if all four use it, prefer three days/week. Assistance
50–100 each; conditioning three hard and 3–5 easy days.

## The Morning Star

**Leader.** A highly specific squat/press/power-clean strength plan for lifters
who already clean competently. Power snatch, hang snatch, or hang clean may
replace the power clean; deadlift does not. The heading says 85–90% TM, but the
prescriptive prose says **85% or less**. Run 2–3 cycles, three days/week:

- Monday: power clean 10 total reps at TM; squat 5×5 SSL; press 10×5 FSL.
- Wednesday: power clean work to TM×3–5; squat 10 total reps at TM; press 5×5
  SSL.
- Friday: power clean 3×3–5 SSL; squat 10×5 FSL; press 10 total reps at TM.

Ten reps at TM are distributed across any number of crisp sets; stop sets before
speed drops. Friday cleans may instead be 3–5×3 SSL. Use 5/3/1 or 3/5/1.
Assistance: push 0–50, pull 25–50, single-leg/core 0–50. Conditioning: at most
two hard, plus 3–5 easy.

**Anchor.** Keep the same three lifts and make one the PR lift each day:

- Monday: power clean work to TM for a PR; squat 5×5 FSL; press 10 total reps
  at TM.
- Wednesday: power clean 10 total reps at TM; squat work to TM for a PR; press
  5×5 FSL.
- Friday: power clean 5×3–5 FSL; squat 10 total reps at TM; press work to TM
  for a PR.

The clean PR stops at technical breakdown. Assistance 50–100 each;
conditioning three hard and 3–5 easy.

## Volume and Strength

**Leader.** A long three-day full-body strength-biased plan; average for
hypertrophy and not ideal when conditioning is the main goal. Use 85–90% TM,
preferably 85%.

- Monday: squat to TM×3–5; bench BBS 10×5 FSL or 5×5 SSL; rows 100 total.
- Wednesday: squat BBS or SSL; bench to TM×3–5; deadlift to TM×3–5.
- Friday: squat to TM×3–5; bench to TM×3–5; deadlift BBS or SSL.

If squat uses BBS, deadlift should use SSL, or vice versa. Press may replace
bench in either strength or volume slots. Strength work rises in 10% increments
from roughly 40–50%, using 1–5 reps; TM work must stay fast and non-failing.
Assistance 25–50 each; conditioning 2–3 hard and 3–5 easy.

**Anchor.** Keep the layout but use 5×5 FSL for volume and choose strength work
per lift: 5/3/1 PR set; PR + Jokers; work to TM for a PR; or a Spinal Tap wave:
70/80/90%, then 75/85/95%, then optional 100–105% Jokers, with 1–5 reps
(95% is one rep; Jokers 1–3). Use at most 1–2 Jokers. Assistance 50–100 each;
conditioning 2–3 hard and 3–5 easy.

## 5×5/3/1 family

### 5×5/3/1 Leader

For beginner to early-intermediate lifters who need heavy-set volume and can
judge bar speed. Leader only, exactly two Leaders then one Anchor, with an
**80% TM maximum**. Main work:

| Week | Work |
|---|---|
| 1 | 65%×5, 75%×5, then 5×5 at 85% |
| 2 | 70%×5, 80%×5, then 5×5 at 90% |
| 3 | 75%×5, 85%×5, then 5×5 at 95% |

No set should grind; aim to finish the top 5×5 within about 20 minutes. Consider
TM increases of only 5 lb for squat/deadlift and 2.5 lb or fractional plates for
press/bench.

Options include one lift/day; a full-body pairing with another lift at 5×5 FSL;
the same on a three-day nine-day rotation; pairing with 10×5 FSL BBS (prefer
three days and reduce to 5×5 if bar speed suffers); or pairing with an FSL
Widowmaker. One-lift days allow assistance 50–100 each. Full-body pairings
usually use 25–50; the three-day lower-volume variants may use 50–100.
Conditioning: 2–3 hard maximum, 3–5 easy.

### 5×5/3/1 Anchor

Primary wave:

- Week 1: 70%×5, 80%×5, then 5×3 at 90%.
- Week 2: 65%×5, 75%×5, then 5×5 at 85%.
- Week 3: 75%×5, 85%×5, then either 5×2 or 3×3 at 95%.

It can be one lift/day or paired with 5×5 FSL on three- or four-day schedules.
Alternative competitive anchor: week 1 70/80/90×5 plus TM×3–5 (not a PR);
week 2 65/75 and 5×5 at 85%; week 3 70/80/90×5 plus TM PR. Assistance 50–100
each; conditioning 3–4 hard maximum and 3–5 easy. Ordinary FSL-family anchors
may also follow 5×5/3/1.

### Rhodes 5×5/3/1

Intermediate strength/volume variation, 80–85% TM. Each week performs the first
two normal work sets, then **two sets of five at the top percentage followed by
a PR set at that same percentage**: 65/75 then 2×5 + PR at 85%; 70/80 then 2×5
+ PR at 90%; 75/85 then 2×5 + PR at 95%. One set of five before the PR is an
allowed reduction. Use any 5×5/3/1 layout, assistance, conditioning, and anchor.
If five reps cannot be made, the TM is wrong.

### Portal's 5×5/3/1

Leader only for intermediate/advanced strength, 80% TM. Four-day full body:
each day one lift uses 5×5/3/1 while another works to TM×3–5; pairings are
squat/press, bench/deadlift, press/squat, deadlift/bench. Assistance is only
0–25 each; conditioning at most two hard plus 3–5 easy.

Anchor: the first lift uses PR + optional Jokers, the other 5×5 FSL. An
alternative takes the first lift to TM for a PR every week and attempts one more
rep in weeks 2 and 3; this weekly-TM-PR alternative lasts only one cycle (three
weeks). Assistance 25–50 each; conditioning at most three hard plus 3–5 easy.

## Five and Dime

**Leader.** Three-day A/B rotation, always Leader, for lower-level lifters with
sound form who like variety. Use an 80% TM. One lift performs 5×5/3/1 at the
top percentage while the paired lift uses a PR target of ten reps. These are
**per-lift exposures**, not uniform calendar weeks:

- Exposure 1: 5×5 at 85% versus 65%×5, 75%×5, 85%×10+.
- Exposure 2: 5×5 at 90% versus 70%×5, 80%×5, 90%×10+.
- Exposure 3: repeat exposure 2.
- Exposure 4: 5×5 at 95% versus 75%×5, 85%×5, 95%×10+.

The three-day A/B rotation distributes this sequence asynchronously: different
lifts occupy different exposures within the same calendar week. The complete
cycle still lasts four weeks. Run only two cycles. Ten is the goal; routinely
reaching only six or seven means the TM is too high. Assistance 25–50 each;
conditioning 2–3 hard and 3–5 easy.

**Anchor.** Same four-week rotation and PR prescriptions, but every 5×5 slot is
held at **85%** throughout. Back it down if speed falls. Assistance 50–100 each;
conditioning three hard and 3–5 easy.

## Simplest Strength Template (SST)

Leader or Anchor, 85–90% TM, and a useful higher-variety follow-up to BBB.
Original supplemental wave:

- Week 1: 50%×10, 60%×10, 70%×10.
- Week 2: 60%×10, 70%×8, 80%×6.
- Week 3: 65%×5, 75%×5, 85%×5.

The more intensive, younger-lifter variation repeats the top set for three sets:
3×10 at 70%, 3×6 at 80%, 3×5 at 85%.

As Leader, pair 5's PRO main work with SST for 2–3 cycles; assistance 25–50 and
conditioning two hard/3–5 easy. As Anchor, pair 5/3/1 PR sets with SST;
assistance 50–100 and conditioning three hard/3–5 easy. BBB or 5's PRO + 5×5 FSL
are natural Leaders before the anchor.

## God Is a Beast

**Leader.** Two long six-week cycles only, for patient lifters seeking size,
strength, and work capacity who have ample time and recovery. Use an 85% TM.
It alternates a Spinal Tap “strength” session for a lift with BBS for the other
pair:

- Strength 1: 70/80/90×5, then 75/85/95×5.
- Strength 2: 65/75/85×5, then 70/80/90×5.
- Strength 3: 70/80/90×5, then 80/90×5, 100%×1–3.

Weeks 1–2 use Strength 1 and 10×5 at 65%; weeks 3–4 Strength 2 and 10×5 at 70%;
weeks 5–6 Strength 3 and 10×5 at 75%. Squat/deadlift take the strength slots in
odd weeks and BBS in even weeks; bench/press do the reverse. Raise TMs after week
6, then repeat once. Main reps must be precise, not difficult. Assistance 25–50
each and may be supersetted; conditioning 2–3 hard maximum (zero is allowed)
plus 3–5 easy.

**Anchor.** One or two cycles. Spinal Tap work uses mostly triples:

- Week 1: 70%×3, 80%×3, 90%×3+; then 75%×3, 85%×3, 95%×3+.
- Week 2: 65%×3, 75%×3, 85%×3; then 70%×3, 80%×3, 90%×3+.
- Week 3: 70%×3, 80%×3, 90%×3+; then 80%×3, 90%×3, 100%×1–3+.

Take at most one PR opportunity even though several sets are marked `+`.
Opposite slots use 5×5 FSL: standard 65/70/75, or 3/5/1 70/65/75.
Assistance stays 25–50 each; conditioning remains 2–3 hard maximum and 3–5
easy.

## Full Body, Four Days

**Leader.** Four-day full-body strength plan, not a size specialization. Use an
85% TM for 2–3 cycles; requires time, recovery, and restraint. Pair:

- Monday: squat 5×5 SSL; bench work to TM×1–3 or 5/3/1 5's PRO.
- Tuesday: deadlift to TM×1–3 or 5's PRO; press 5×5 SSL.
- Thursday: squat to TM×1–3 or 5's PRO; bench 5×5 SSL.
- Friday: deadlift 5×5 SSL; press to TM×1–3 or 5's PRO.

Assistance 50–100 each, but reduce push volume if main pressing suffers.
Conditioning 2–3 hard maximum (zero allowed) plus 3–5 easy.

**Anchor.** Keep the paired layout; strength slots may use 5/3/1 PR, PR +
Jokers, or work to TM for a PR, while opposite slots use 5×5 FSL. Jokers only
when readiness warrants. Assistance 50–100 each; conditioning 3–4 hard and 3–5
easy.

## Black Army Jacket

**Leader.** Experienced, well-recovered strength lifters only; four-day full
body for 2–3 cycles with an 85% TM. The Spinal Tap lift alternates with another
lift at 5×5 FSL:

- Monday squat Spinal Tap, bench FSL.
- Tuesday deadlift FSL, press Spinal Tap.
- Thursday squat FSL, bench Spinal Tap.
- Friday deadlift Spinal Tap, press FSL.

Spinal Tap prescription: 70/80/90% for 3,3,1–3; 75/85/95% for 3,3,1–3;
65/75/85% for 3,3,3. Use 5's PRO instead of Spinal Tap on lifts not being
pushed. TM increases only after the complete three-week cycle, never after an
individual exposure. Assistance 25–50 each and can be done between work sets.
Conditioning 2–3 hard maximum (zero allowed) and 3–5 easy.

**Anchor.** Spinal Tap on one lift each day, with optional PR and/or Jokers after
the 90%, 95%, or 85% set, selected by bar speed and experience. Other lifts may
switch to 5's PRO + 5×5 FSL. Assistance 50–100 each; conditioning 3–4 hard and
3–5 easy.

## Spinal Tap variants

### Spinal Tap, 5's PRO

Leader for 2–3 cycles, 85% TM, for reasonably experienced lifters who can handle
nine work sets and full-body assistance. Run three days, four days, or every
other day; repeat each lift three times before increasing TM. Prescription:
70/80/90×5, 75/85/95×5, 65/75/85×5, with no extra reps. It may be used on only
selected lifts while the others use 5's PRO + 5×5 FSL. Finish warm-ups, work,
and assistance in roughly 60 minutes.

Assistance must be supersetted. For press/bench, across about 12 warm-up/work
sets: face pulls/band pull-aparts; one-leg squats; rows; KB swings/snatches. For
squat/deadlift: ab wheel; dips/push-ups; alternating chins and dips/push-ups.
Scale reps to ability. Conditioning 2–3 hard maximum plus 3–5 easy.

**Anchor.** Use Spinal Tap with triples:
70/80/90×3, 75/85/95×3, 65/75/85×3. The final set of each wave may be a PR and/or
followed by 1–2 Jokers; normally emphasize the 95% opportunity. Assistance
unchanged; conditioning 3–4 hard and 3–5 easy.

### Spinal Tap, High School Years

Two lifting days and two assistance/conditioning days for younger or
non-advanced lifters with prior training, coaching, limited gym time, and good
work capacity. Use an 85% TM. Monday superset squat and bench Spinal Tap 5's
PRO; Thursday superset deadlift and press. Pairings may switch to squat/press
and deadlift/bench.

At-home assistance at least twice and up to five times/week: bodyweight squat
100, push-ups 50, chins 25, sit-ups/leg raises 100. Conditioning 2–3 hard and
3–5 easy.

Anchor option 1 changes every Spinal Tap set to three reps, with optional PR or
Jokers at 90/95/85. Option 2 uses:
70×3, 80×3, 90×3; 75×5, 85×3, 95×1; 65×5, 75×5, 85×5. PR/Jokers may be used at
the wave tops; the author prefers five at 95% when speed is good, then 1–2
Jokers, and normally does not push the 85% top. Assistance frequency remains
2–5 days; jumps rise to 20 per lifting day.

## Coffinworm

**Leader.** Heavy, simplified work for intermediate/advanced lifters, 85–90%
TM, two cycles, three or four days/week. Weeks 1 and 2 are identical:
70/80/90×5, then 80%×5, 90%×3–5, 100%×1–3. Week 3 is a semi-deload:
65/75×5, then 3×5 at 85%. In weeks 1–2 only, pair the main Coffinworm lift with
another lift at 5×5 at 70%; week 3 has no paired supplemental lift. Assistance
25–50 each; conditioning 2–3 hard maximum plus 3–5 easy. Because week 3 is a
natural deload, the book allows skipping the 7th Week deload before its anchor.

**Anchor.** Same schedule. In weeks 1–2, optional paired supplemental work is
5×5 at 70%; week 3 has none. Weeks 1 and 2: 70/80/90×5, then 80%×5,
90%×3–5, TM PR, optional Jokers. Week 3: 65/75×5 then 85% PR. Assistance and
conditioning remain 25–50 each and 2–3 hard/3–5 easy.

## Second Set Last (SSL)

Leader for 2–3 cycles, 85–90% TM, easy to pair with FSL and compatible with
meaningful assistance/conditioning. Normally use 5's PRO main work followed by
5×5 at SSL:

- Week 1: second set 80%.
- Week 2: second set 75%.
- Week 3: second set 85%.

Use 5/3/1 or 3/5/1 and train three or four days/week. Full-body options put 5's
PRO on one exposure and SSL/FSL supplemental work on another. A high-volume
variant pairs SSL with opposite-lift BBS; it requires no hard conditioning and
only curls, chins/pull-ups, and face pulls between press/bench work. A milder
version replaces BBS with 5×5 FSL. Assistance is normally 25–50 each.
Conditioning 2–3 hard and 3–5 easy, except remove hard conditioning with
SSL/BBS.

## Full Body, 85%

Leader for 2–3 cycles, 85% TM, for trained lifters who understand bar speed and
want heavy full-body supplemental work. Main work must be 5's PRO. Four-day
layout alternates:

- Monday squat 5's PRO, bench 5×5 at 85%.
- Tuesday deadlift 5×5 at 85%, press 5's PRO.
- Thursday squat 5×5 at 85%, bench 5's PRO.
- Friday deadlift 5's PRO, press 5×5 at 85%.

Three days/week is advisable if all lifts use it. If only two use 85%, keep the
other two at 5×5 FSL. Assistance 50–100 each; conditioning 2–3 hard maximum plus
3–5 easy.

The BBS combination replaces Tuesday deadlift and Friday press with 10×5 FSL;
assistance drops to 25–50 each and hard conditioning to two days. Follow with a
normal FSL-family anchor.

## Boring But Strong (BBS)

**Leader.** Strength/high-volume template for nearly all levels, 2–3 cycles,
usually with an 85% TM. Use 5's PRO plus 10×5 at FSL (65%, 70%, 75% in 5/3/1
order). 3/5/1 is allowed. Maintain fast reps or increase rest/lower TM. It is
often easier to recover from than BBB, though squat/deadlift remain stressful.

### BBS Challenge

Three cycles, starting at an 85–90% TM:

- Cycle 1: 5's PRO + 10×5 at 60%; assistance 50–100 each.
- Cycle 2: 5's PRO + 10×5 at 70%; assistance 50–100 each.
- Cycle 3: 5's PRO + 10×5 at 80%; assistance 0–50 each.

Raise TM between cycles. Alternative: within each of three cycles, week 1 uses
10×5 at 60%, week 2 at 70%, week 3 at 80%; assistance follows 50–100, 50–100,
0–50 by week.

### BBS variants

- **NTW:** Monday/Tuesday are 5's PRO for two lifts each; Thursday/Friday perform
  two BBS lifts each. Assistance 50–100 on Mon/Tue, 0–50 Thu/Fri. Rarely, a
  young/less experienced lifter may add PRs early in the week; not recommended
  after 8–10 consistent years.
- **The Whip and the Body:** emphasize two lifts, each with BBS once and SSL
  once; the other two lifts receive only 5's PRO main work. In the printed
  example, squat gets BBS Monday and SSL Thursday, press gets SSL Tuesday and
  BBS Friday, and bench/deadlift get 5's PRO only. Assistance 0–50.
- **Negative Birth:** BBS on two selected lifts twice/week. Highly stressful to
  back/shoulders; avoid deadlift emphasis if it degrades squat. Assistance 0–50.
- **At the Mouth:** combine BBS for two lifts and BBB for two. Put BBB late in
  the week for recovery. Intended only for relatively new lifters. BBB can use
  any valid BBB percentage, including FSL with a light TM. Assistance 0–50.
- **FSL/BBS:** two lifts use BBS; two use 5's PRO + 5×5 FSL. BBS days use
  assistance 0–50; FSL days 50–100.

All BBS variants: at most two hard conditioning days and at least four total
conditioning days including easy work. Follow 2–3 Leaders with an FSL-family
anchor.

## Supplemental Heaven

Use an alternate supplemental lift to freshen long-term training and build a
closely related weakness. Examples are floor press, incline press, front squat,
and deficit deadlift. Establish a separate TM for each alternate movement, raise
it normally after cycles, and retain it for at least two cycles. An alternate
movement with its own TM may replace same-lift supplemental work in any
compatible template, explicitly including BBB, BBS, FSL, and SSL.

The hard-push example uses 5×5/3/1 for the alternate movement. It begins a new
four-to-five-cycle program and is recommended for three Leader cycles, although
two are allowed. The main lift uses 5's PRO or works to TM×3–5 with an 85–90%
main-lift TM; the alternate supplemental movement uses 5×5/3/1 with an 80% TM.
Same-lift and full-body pairings are both shown. Assistance 25–50 each;
conditioning 2–3 hard and 3–5 easy, avoiding novel conditioning stress. Carry
the same supplemental movements into the following FSL-family Anchor.

## Pervertor

**Leader.** Size/strength variety template, three or four days/week (nine-day
rotation if recovery demands), 2–3 cycles, **85% TM**, and mandatory 3/5/1.
Every main lift uses 5's PRO:

- Week 1: BBS 10×5 at FSL (70%).
- Week 2: BBB 5×10 at FSL (65%), or a lower valid BBB percentage if needed.

**Anchor.** 3/5/1:

- Week 1: main work with PR + up to 1–2 Jokers, then 1×20 FSL at 70%.
- Week 2: 5's PRO, then 5×5 SSL at 75%.
- Week 3: main work with PR + up to 1–2 Jokers, then 5×5 FSL at 75%.

Twenty is a Widowmaker goal, not permission for degraded form. Assistance rises
to 50–100 each; conditioning 3–4 hard and 3–5 easy.

## Original 5/3/1

**Purpose and fit.** The original program emphasizes PR sets, simple programming, and the mental pressure of setting rep records. It is well suited to lifters who enjoy PR work and want room for substantial conditioning. Standard main work is:

- Week 1: 65%×5, 75%×5, 85%×5+.
- Week 2: 70%×3, 80%×3, 90%×3+.
- Week 3: 75%×5, 85%×3, 95%×1+.

Train squat, bench press, deadlift, and press once each over four days; bench/press and squat/deadlift days may be swapped. Push the final set for a rep or estimated-max PR. After the cycle, raise the lower-body TMs by 10 lb and upper-body TMs by 5 lb only when the TM remains correct.

Ways to organize longer phases:

- For the first 2–3 cycles, use 100 reps each of push, pull, and single-leg/core per workout; for the final 2–3 cycles, reduce each category to 50–75. Main work, jumps/throws, and conditioning remain unchanged.
- A main-work variation uses week 1: 65%×10, 75%×5, 85%×5+; week 2: 70%×10, 80%×5, 90%×3+; week 3: 75%×10, 85%×5, 95%×1+. Aim for at least 10 reps on the final set, use a correct TM, run 2–3 cycles, then return to normal 5/3/1. Assistance stays 50–100 reps/category.
- A three-day A/B option alternates squat+bench and deadlift+press in an A/B/A then B/A/B sequence. The main-work weeks continue in order, so each lift is trained three times over two weeks. This is more intensive and not for everyone. Run 2–3 cycles, then return to original 5/3/1. Assistance is normally 50–100 reps/category; a high-assistance Leader followed by sharply reduced assistance is possible but demanding.
- The closest conventional Leader is 2–3 cycles of 5/3/1 Widowmaker followed by original 5/3/1 as the Anchor.

Do 10–20 total jumps/throws per workout: use the high end with one main lift per day and the low end with two, although capable beginners/intermediates may tolerate the high end. Up to four hard-conditioning days are possible, but build into them. Prefer hard conditioning on lifting days and recovery work on off days. Reduce assistance when conditioning is pushed.

## Original 5/3/1 and First Set Last

**Leader.** Simple, PR-oriented, and conditioning-friendly; use an 85–90% TM. Use 3/5/1:

- Week 1: 70%×3, 80%×3, 90%×3+ PR; then 5×5 at 70%.
- Week 2: 65%×5, 75%×5, 85%×5 with no PR set; then 5×5 at 65%.
- Week 3: 75%×5, 85%×3, 95%×1+ PR; then 5×5 at 75%.

Perform 20 jumps/throws on each of the four lifting days. FSL may be reduced from 5×5 to 3×5 when conditioning or recovery demands it; every rep stays fast, controlled, and explosive. Assistance is 50–100 reps each of push, pull, and single-leg/core. Conditioning is 3–4 hard and 3–5 easy sessions weekly. If conditioning is demanding, reduce assistance to 25–50 reps/category and/or use 3×5 FSL.

**Anchor.** Use original 5/3/1 with either standard 5/3/1 or 3/5/1. With 3/5/1, PRs may be limited to weeks 1 and 3 or taken all three weeks. Standard 5/3/1 uses PRs each week. Assistance remains 50–100/category; conditioning remains 3–4 hard and 3–5 easy days. Reduce assistance or limit PRs to weeks 1 and 3 if conditioning is the priority.

## 5/3/1 Prowler Challenge

**Purpose and fit.** A Leader challenge specifically paired with original 5/3/1, not other templates. It aims to preserve big-lift PRs while reaching the gym standard of ten 40-yard Prowler pushes with bodyweight in 15 minutes. It is not aimed at powerlifters or lifters centered on a 1RM.

Run three cycles (nine training weeks), four lifting days/week. A deload after week 6 is optional. Use roughly 75% of bodyweight on the Prowler throughout training, adjusted for the Prowler and surface.

- Weeks 1–3: after mobility, 10–20 jumps/throws, and the main-lift PR set, complete six circuits of a 40-yard Prowler push, 10–20 push reps, and 5–20 pull reps. Rest as needed; learn the workload rather than racing it.
- Weeks 4–6: complete the same six circuits with about one minute between exercises, rest five minutes, then complete four more circuits with whatever rest is needed. One weekly circuit day may become 20–30 easy/steady AirDyne minutes, with ordinary assistance after the main lift.
- Weeks 7–8: complete ten circuits with about 90 seconds between exercises. One weekly circuit day may again become 20–30 minutes on the AirDyne.
- Week 9: test ten 40-yard pushes at bodyweight in 15 minutes, usually on a bench or press day.

Use familiar movements safe under fatigue: push-ups, dips, or light dumbbell pressing; chin-ups/pull-ups, rows, or pulldowns. Bodyweight work is usually safest. Support the challenge with consistent food, hydration, sleep, and morning/evening mobility.

## Original 5/3/1 Challenge

**Purpose and fit.** A complete four-cycle program combining PR sets, running, Prowler work, and bodyweight assistance. It requires competence with bodyweight exercises and access to a weight vest or dip belt. Begin with an 85% TM.

The schedule is a three-day A/B rotation. Every listed lifting day begins with 10 jumps/throws, and every 3×5, 3×3, or 5/3/1 exposure includes a PR set:

- Week 1: squat+bench 3×5; deadlift+press 3×5; squat+bench 3×3.
- Week 2: deadlift+press 3×3; squat+bench 5/3/1; deadlift+press 5/3/1.
- Week 3: 7th Week Protocol deload.
- Weeks 4–5 repeat weeks 1–2 with the next cycle's TMs.
- Week 6: 7th Week TM test; obtain at least 3 reps with each TM.

The first two cycles are Leaders. Use unweighted assistance each workout: 100 push-ups or dips, 75 chin-ups/pull-ups, and 50 ab-wheel reps or back raises (alternate the core movement). Run 1600 m total Tuesday and Thursday using intervals from the running protocol. Saturday: 10×40-yard Prowler pushes at 75% bodyweight.

The next two cycles are Anchors using the same A/B main-lift rotation and deload/TM-test placement. Assistance becomes weighted with 10% bodyweight: 50 push-ups/dips, 50 chin-ups/pull-ups, and 50 ab-wheel reps/back raises. Run 2400 m Tuesday and Thursday. Saturday: 15×40-yard Prowler pushes at 75% bodyweight.

## Combination Template

**Purpose and fit.** A Leader for experienced lifters who know which supplemental work drives each lift. It offers strength/size variety and may require a different TM for every lift. Run 2–3 cycles.

Choose per lift among BBB, BBS, SSL, FSL, 5×5/3/1, and Widowmaker work. Put the hardest-to-recover work late in the week. Example:

- Deadlift: 5's PRO + 5×5 SSL, 85% TM.
- Squat: PR set + 1×20 FSL Widowmaker, 85% TM.
- Bench: 5×5/3/1 with no extra supplemental work, 80% TM.
- Press: 5's PRO + 10×5 FSL, 85–90% TM.

Another useful pairing is BBB for squat/bench (5's PRO + 5×10 no heavier than FSL) and BBS for deadlift/press (5's PRO + 10×5 FSL). Assistance is 25–50 reps/category. Conditioning is two hard and 3–5 easy days.

There is no single Anchor because the Leader can vary so much. Suitable Anchors include 5's PRO+Jokers+FSL, PR set+FSL, PR set+Jokers+FSL, 5/3/1+Widowmaker, 5's PRO+FSL, full-body 5's PRO or PR-set programs, Beyond FSL, and original 5/3/1.

## Limited Time

**Purpose and fit.** For lifters with demanding work/family schedules. Allocate focused training most days, generally no more than 40 minutes and sometimes only 15. Aim weekly for two lifting days, two assistance/conditioning days, and two mobility/jump sessions. Use an alternating A/B structure if the calendar is irregular. Always preserve the warm-up.

Run 4–5 total cycles: 2–3 Leaders followed by enough Anchors to total 4–5 cycles. Each two-day Leader trains squat+bench on one day and deadlift+press on the other, followed by Kroc rows:

- **Widowmaker:** PR set + Widowmaker for all four lifts.
- **BBS:** choose one lower and one upper lift on different days; work each to a TM single, then 10×5 FSL. The other two lifts use PR sets.
- **BBB:** choose one lower and one upper lift; work each to a TM single, then 5×10 at or below FSL. The other two use PR sets.
- **BBB/BBS:** use BBB for one lift and BBS for another, never stacking both hard supplementals carelessly.
- **5×5/3/1:** use it for one lower and one upper lift, with an 80% TM; the other two use PR sets.

Kroc rows: after 1–2 warm-ups, do one hard set of roughly 20–50 reps/arm, normally aiming for 30+. Standards are one-half bodyweight for 20 beginner, 35 intermediate, or 50 advanced reps/arm.

The Anchor uses PR sets for all four lifts over the two days, plus Kroc rows.

On separate assistance/jump days, warm up, perform 10–20 jumps/throws, then a circuit of push, pull, and single-leg/core work. Use at least 50–100 reps/category, adjusted to ability. Favor push-ups/dips; chin-ups/pull-ups/inverted rows; and back raises, hanging leg raises, ab wheel, kettlebell swings/snatches, or single-leg work. A weight vest is optional. Do two weekly conditioning sessions of 20–30 minutes, hard or easy according to recovery.

## Bodybuild the Upper / Athlete the Lower

**Purpose and fit.** For athletes or physical-test candidates needing upper-body size/strength plus running and lower-body capacity. Use an 85% TM. Not for beginners or people without a running/jumping history.

**Leader, 2–3 cycles:**

- Monday: press 5's PRO + 5×10 FSL; 10 jumps; 1600 m running.
- Tuesday: squat 5's PRO + 3×5 FSL; 20 jumps; Prowler 10×40 yards at 75% bodyweight.
- Thursday: bench 5's PRO + 5×10 FSL; 10 jumps; 1600 m running.
- Friday: deadlift 5's PRO + 10×5 FSL; 20 jumps; sled 5×40 forward and 5×40 backward at 75% bodyweight.
- Wednesday/Sunday: 20 minutes AirDyne plus recovery/mobility. Saturday: 2400 m running.

Upper-focused Monday/Thursday assistance is 25–50 reps/category; lower-focused Tuesday/Friday assistance is 50–100/category.

**Anchor, 1–2 cycles:** Press on Monday and bench press on Thursday use 5's PRO + 4×8 FSL. The printed Anchor table repeats “Press” on Thursday, but that is an obvious source-book typo: it would otherwise omit bench, and the Leader establishes press Monday/bench Thursday. Squat and deadlift use 5/3/1 PR sets with no supplemental work Tuesday/Friday. Keep 10 jumps/throws Monday/Thursday and 20 Tuesday/Friday. Monday running rises to 2400 m; Thursday remains 1600 m; Saturday rises to 3600 m. Prowler, sled, AirDyne, and assistance targets remain as above. Assistance must not interfere with running.

## Strength and Conditioning

**Purpose and fit.** A seven-calendar-week, seven-day schedule for experienced lifters/runners, athletes, law enforcement, or comprehensive physical tests. The source labels five program weeks, but its required sequence is four Leader weeks (the two-week rotation repeated for two cycles), one deload week, and two Anchor weeks. It develops running, barbell strength, push-ups, and pull-ups. Use an 85% TM and do not begin without a lifting and running base. Prowler/sled percentages include the implement and must be adjusted for surface.

**Two Leader cycles:** Lift Monday/Wednesday/Friday in an A/B rotation. Squat and deadlift use 5's PRO + 3×5 SSL; bench and press use 5's PRO + 5×5 SSL. Perform 10 jumps/throws. Finish squat days with Prowler 4×50 at 75% bodyweight and deadlift days with sled 2×50 forward + 2×50 backward at 75%. Run 1600 m Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday; Sunday is 20 minutes AirDyne plus recovery/mobility.

On bench days, do a 10-minute push-up max-reps test, plus 25–50 pull and 25–50 single-leg/core reps. On press days, do a 10-minute pull-up max-reps test, plus 25–50 push and 25–50 single-leg/core reps.

After two cycles, use a 7th Week deload: 0–25 reps/category, Prowler/sled at 50% bodyweight, 1600 m runs Tuesday/Thursday/Saturday, and Sunday off.

**Anchor:** Adjust TMs. Over two alternating weeks, use PR sets for the paired lower and upper lifts; the upper lift also gets 3×5 FSL. Prowler and sled return to 75%. Run 2400 m Tuesday/Thursday and 3600 m Saturday; Sunday is 20 minutes AirDyne plus recovery/mobility. Every lifting day includes a five-minute push-up test, five-minute pull-up test, and 25–50 single-leg/core reps.

## Wendler Classic and WaLRUS

**Purpose and fit.** A balanced program for older/experienced lifters who prioritize strength, speed, conditioning, joint comfort, and quality of life over maximizing a 1RM. Workouts are generally under an hour. Run two Leaders, a 7th Week deload, and one Anchor.

**Leader:** Two barbell days, three weight-vest circuit days, two jump/throw days, daily mobility, and at least 30 AirDyne miles/week. Monday is squat+bench; Thursday is deadlift+press, all with 5×5/3/1 and no assistance. TM is no higher than 80%, preferably lower. The standard ramp is:

- Week 1: 65%×5, 75%×5, then five sets of 5 at 85%.
- Week 2: 70%×5, 80%×5, then five sets of 5 at 90%.
- Week 3: 75%×5, 85%×5, then five sets of 5 at 95%.

Experienced older lifters may use an abbreviated ramp: week 1 55%×5, 70%×5, then 5×5 at 85%; week 2 60%×5, 75%×5, then 5×5 at 90%; week 3 65%×5, 80%×5, then 5×5 at 95%.

Weight-vest work Tuesday/Friday/Saturday combines strength and conditioning. The three WaLRUS tests, each under 30 minutes, are:

1. 50 push-ups, 25 chin-ups, 50 Bulgarian squats/leg.
2. 50 push-ups, 25 chin-ups, 125 bodyweight squats.
3. With a vest equal to 10% bodyweight: 50 push-ups, 25 chin-ups, 50 kettlebell snatches/arm.

For tests 1–2, vest ranks are 25/35/45/55/65/75/85 lb. For test 3, kettlebell ranks are 35/44/53/62/70/80/88 lb. Training is not testing: use circuits, finish within 30 minutes without rushing, begin very light, and keep every rep fast and clean. A practical target is to train consistently with 10% bodyweight. Dips can sometimes replace push-ups; grip may vary on chins. Test only during the deload if desired.

Ride the AirDyne in 20–30 minute bouts to total 30 miles/week. Work hard enough to train but never turn it into a test. Do mobility daily.

**Anchor:** Two barbell days using original 5/3/1 PR sets for squat+bench and deadlift+press. Assistance is 25–100 reps/category, emphasizing rows for pull and back raises/reverse hypers/abdominals for core; omit the Leader's high chin-up and single-leg emphasis. Tuesday/Friday/Saturday use sled or Prowler for 20–30 minutes, training rather than testing. Continue daily mobility and 30 AirDyne miles/week. End with a 7th Week TM check; 80% remains the maximum TM, not a target.

## Leviathan

**Purpose and fit.** Strength-focused and potentially taxing. Use an 85–90% TM, lower for more experienced or faster lifters. Every workout in both Leader and Anchor works through 70%, 80%, and 90% for 1–3 reps each, then takes 100% TM for one clean single.

**Leader:** Perform 10 jumps/throws, then the ramp and TM single. Choose per lift: 10×5 FSL, 5×5 FSL, 5×10 FSL, 5×5 SSL, or 1×20 FSL. Sequence the most stressful/soreness-producing lifts late in the week. Not every lift must be pushed; 3–5×5 FSL can deliberately reduce stress. Assistance is 25–50/category, but remove lower-back and single-leg work when squat/deadlift supplemental volume is high. Conditioning: two hard and 3–5 easy days.

**Anchor:** Perform 20 jumps/throws, ramp to the TM single, then perform the week's PR set: 85% in week 1, 90% in week 2, 95% in week 3. Assistance rises to 50–100/category. Conditioning: 3–4 hard and 3–5 easy days.

## Con Clavi Con Dio

**Purpose and fit.** For older/advanced lifters dedicated to training outside the weight room. Each week has two lifting days, two conditioning days, and two flexibility/mobility circuit days.

**Leader:** Monday squat+bench; Thursday deadlift+press. For each lift choose either 5×5/3/1 (TM no higher than 80%, often 70–75% for advanced lifters) or 5's PRO + 3–5×5 FSL. Mix choices per lift with appropriate TMs. Do no jumps or assistance on lifting days. Usually run two Leaders then one Anchor; because lifting is only twice weekly, the deload may be omitted, but older lifters should take it when needed.

**Anchor:** Original 5/3/1 PR sets for all four lifts across the two days. A 3/5/1 option may limit week 2 to minimum reps. No jumps or assistance on lifting days.

Wednesday/Saturday combine assistance, mobility, and jumps/throws. Build 3–4 distinct four-exercise circuits and perform each circuit three times. Each circuit should include at least one jump/throw, one bodyweight or kettlebell movement, one mobility drill, and a flexible fourth movement such as rope or AirDyne. Move briskly but never become exhausted; mobility is controlled, bodyweight reps perfect, and jumps explosive.

Tuesday/Friday conditioning combines 10–20 minutes of hard sled/Prowler work with 30+ minutes of easy vest walking or AirDyne. Neither should become a test. These conditioning and mobility prescriptions remain unchanged from Leader to Anchor.

## Prep and Fat Loss Training

**Purpose and fit.** A six-week preparatory/Leader phase for experienced lifters needing work capacity, general fitness, or gradual fat loss without abandoning strength. It is not for beginners. Lift four days/week with an 85% TM, 10 jumps/throws, 5's PRO, and FSL. Use 3/5/1:

- Week 1: 70%×5, 80%×5, 90%×5; then 5×5 at 70%.
- Week 2: 65%×5, 75%×5, 85%×5; then 5×5 at 65%.
- Week 3: 75%×5, 85%×5, 95%×5; then 5×5 at 75%.

Do not chase PRs. Repeat for weeks 4–6, increasing supplemental work from five to seven sets during the final three weeks.

Perform assistance between every warm-up, main, and supplemental set. On bench/press days, interleave rows or chins plus examples such as face pulls/band pull-aparts, bodyweight Bulgarian squats or lunges, and kettlebell swings. Kettlebell snatches may replace swings only during bench FSL sets, for 10/arm. On squat/deadlift days, alternate abdominal work, dips/push-ups, and chin-ups/inverted rows. Bodyweight movements are not weighted. Adjust assistance reps to ability; technique and safety outrank pace.

Rest comfortably, no more than about 90 seconds between exercises, potentially 60 seconds after adaptation. Finish lifting in roughly 45 minutes without turning it into conditioning.

Do 2–4 conditioning days, preferably easy: 2–3 mile vest walks, 30-minute AirDyne, or 1–2 mile runs for prepared runners. If using Prowler, use roughly 50–75% bodyweight for 10–12×40 yards in 15–20 minutes without exhaustion. Suitable Anchors include PR/5's PRO variations with FSL and/or Jokers, 5/3/1 Widowmakers, full-body programs, Beyond FSL, or original 5/3/1.

## 5/3/1 Strength Circuits

**Purpose and fit.** Only for highly advanced lifters already in excellent aerobic shape, with perfect technique and the judgment to set per-lift TMs. It is not strength-specialization programming and not for beginners. TM must not exceed 80%.

**Leader, 1–2 cycles (the author personally prefers two), followed by 1–2 Anchors:** Use 5×5/3/1, standard or abbreviated. Alternate:

- A: squat + bench + row for 5×10.
- B: deadlift + press + five sets of weighted chin-ups/pull-ups, reps chosen by the lifter.

Run A/B/A then B/A/B. Warm up and perform 10 jumps/throws first. Perform five circuits and no additional assistance. Initially time but do not race; once adapted, aim to finish the five work circuits in 30 minutes.

**Anchor, 1–2 cycles:** Each main lift gets its own day in an alternating four-day sequence. Two options:

- PR option: normal 5/3/1 PR set, then one 10–20-rep FSL set.
- “Five and Dime” option: all main work sets stop at 5 reps, followed by one 10-rep FSL set.

Each main-lift set is circuit-paired with two assistance movements, so there are four work sets of each assistance exercise. Squat/deadlift days use one push and one pull; avoid rows on deadlift days if grip/lower-back fatigue is a problem. Bench/press days use two single-leg/core movements or one single-leg/core plus one pull. No work follows the circuit. PR/Widowmaker circuits are not raced; Five and Dime may target 40–45 minutes.

Conditioning is individual. New circuit trainees should remove hard conditioning and use easy conditioning or active recovery. Track sleep, resting heart rate, daily/workout exhaustion, and mental clarity.

## 5's PRO Forever

**Purpose and fit.** For lifters worn down by PR sets or testing which supplemental volume helps each lift. Main work is 5's PRO (or 5×5/3/1 for selected lifts), while each lift independently uses BBB, BBS, FSL, SSL, or Widowmaker supplemental work. TM therefore varies by lift.

**Leader:** Run two cycles with 10 jumps/throws per workout. Example: bench 5's PRO+5×5 SSL; press 5's PRO+5×10 BBB; squat 5×5/3/1; deadlift 5's PRO+5×5 FSL. Assistance depends on supplemental stress; a baseline is 25 push, 25–50 pull, and 25–50 single-leg/core reps. Reduce pressing assistance after high-volume bench/press work and lower-body assistance after high-volume squat/deadlift. Do no more than two hard-conditioning sessions and 3–5 total conditioning days.

After two Leaders, use the 7th Week PR test rather than a deload to evaluate the choices.

**Anchor:** All lifts use 5's PRO + 5×5 FSL with 20 jumps/throws per workout. Assistance may rise to 50–100/category when conditioning is comfortable. Conditioning may be 2–4 hard and 3–5 easy days when goals and recovery permit.

## Titanium Knickknack Challenge

**Purpose and fit.** A deliberately extreme advanced-only challenge requiring strength, conditioning, health, technical control under fatigue, a safety-squat bar, trap bar, and weight vest. It is not for maximizing main-lift strength.

Final test, under 30 minutes while wearing a 10%-bodyweight vest: 25 total reps with trap-bar deadlift or safety-bar squat at 2× bodyweight, 50 push-ups or dips, and 25 chin-ups/pull-ups.

- **Part 1, two cycles:** four lifting days of 5's PRO+5×5 FSL at roughly 80–85% TM, using trap bar and safety bar. Follow each with WaLRUS assistance under 30 minutes. Use a 10%-bodyweight vest in cycle 1 and 20% in cycle 2. Off days are light conditioning/active recovery, not rest or hard work.
- **Part 2, 1–2 cycles:** TM no higher than 80%. Three weekly lifting circuits alternate safety-bar squat+bench+row and trap-bar deadlift+press+weighted chin, using the Strength Circuit 5×5/3/1 format and aiming for 30 minutes of work sets. Three alternate days use WaLRUS; Sunday may be off/easy recovery.
- **Part 3, two cycles:** remove bench and press. Three weekly circuits perform all 85/90/95% barbell work while wearing a 10%-bodyweight vest; lower/adjust the TM to account for the vest and start lighter than expected. Week 1: safety-bar squat 5×5 at 85%, trap-bar deadlift 5×5 at 85%, safety-bar squat 5×5 at 90%. Week 2: trap-bar deadlift 5×5 at 90%, safety-bar squat 5×5 at 95%, trap-bar deadlift 5×5 at 95%. Each session also totals 50 push-ups/dips and 25 chins/pulls, normally as five circuits of 10 push, 5 barbell reps, and 5 pull. Finish in 30 minutes. Alternate days use easy WaLRUS with a 10% vest.

After Part 3, test without taking many complete days off; easy WaLRUS can maintain readiness.

## Widowmaker Circuit

**Purpose and fit.** A time-efficient, balanced, hard program for strength. Use an 85–90% TM. Best as two Leaders and one Anchor. It may be done four days/week or rotated over three days, training each lift every nine rather than seven days.

**Leader:** Each day uses warm-up, 10 jumps/throws, one main-lift PR set, and one FSL Widowmaker. Then perform a five-round assistance circuit totaling about 50–100 reps/category. Use safe, familiar exercises. Example per round: 15 dips, 10 chin-ups, 10 double-kettlebell cleans; optional jump-rope intervals may be added. A vest around 10% bodyweight can be introduced only after adaptation. Establish a natural completion time before trying to improve it. Conditioning is 2–4 easy days only.

**Anchor:** Remove the Widowmaker, keep the PR set, increase jumps/throws to 20, and retain the assistance circuit. Conditioning remains 2–4 easy days.

Use one exact Leader/Anchor pairing:

- Leader 5×5/3/1 + circuits; Anchor chooses one of 5/3/1 PR sets, 5's PRO + 5×5 FSL, or 5/3/1 Widowmaker + circuits.
- Leader 5's PRO + 5×5 FSL + circuits; Anchor chooses one of 5's PRO + 5×5 FSL, 5/3/1 PR sets, or 5/3/1 Widowmaker + circuits.
- Leader 5's PRO + 5×5 SSL + circuits; Anchor uses the same choices as the preceding pairing.

## Ceremony of Opposites

**Purpose and fit.** A phase structure for experienced lifters who can judge recovery. Insert a maintenance phase before Leader/Anchor work. Possible four-cycle sequences are Maintenance/Leader/Leader/Anchor, Maintenance/Maintenance/Leader/Anchor, or Maintenance/Leader/Anchor/Anchor. Lifters without a successful programming history should retain two Leaders and one Anchor.

The maintenance phase uses the lightest usually-static TM that preserves strength and speed while restoring joints, aerobic capacity, mobility, and readiness. A typical prescription is 5's PRO + 3–5×5 FSL, 25–50 assistance reps/category, and easy conditioning only. It is appropriate after a meet or hard training phase.

Intermediate/advanced lifters may use a different supplemental lift in Leaders, then return to the competition/core lift in Anchors. Beginners should keep core lifts. Competitive lifters should become more specific as the meet approaches. Avoid extreme range-of-motion alterations.

## 2×2×2

**Purpose and fit.** Balanced, flexible training for older or time-limited lifters: two lifting, two conditioning, and two dedicated mobility/recovery days each week.

**Leader:** Lift squat+bench on one day and deadlift+press on the other, with 20 jumps/throws each day. Per lift choose 5's PRO plus BBB, BBS, 5×5 SSL, 5×5 FSL, or 1×20 FSL; or use 5×5/3/1 with no additional supplemental work. Mix by lift and set TM accordingly. BBB/BBS can make sessions long and hard to recover from, especially for experienced lifters.

Assistance is governed by supplemental volume. Always include pulling via rows, chins, face pulls, or band pull-aparts, possibly between main sets. Eliminate/drastically reduce pressing assistance after upper-body BBB/BBS; eliminate single-leg and lower-back work after squat/deadlift BBB/BBS, while abdominal work may remain.

**Anchor:** Main lifts use either 5's PRO or 5/3/1 PR sets, followed by 5×5 FSL or no supplemental work. Assistance guideline: 25–50 push, 50–100 pull, and 25–50 single-leg/core reps. Superset intelligently to save time.

Conditioning days serve a specific conditioning goal and need not be hard; easy work may also be added on mobility days. Mobility days deserve at least 45 focused minutes; yoga is acceptable.

## Krypteia

**Purpose and fit.** Builds muscle and strength while often reducing body fat. It requires a training base, reasonable conditioning, perfect technique, little need for variety, and no more than an 85% TM. It is not a powerlifting-strength specialization program and generally does not suit older lifters.

Assistance is paired after every main-lift set, including warm-ups and supplemental sets:

- Bench/press: alternate dumbbell squat and dumbbell straight-leg deadlift with shrug.
- Squat/deadlift: alternate weighted dips and weighted chin-ups/pull-ups. Heavier athletes may use dumbbell incline press and chest-supported incline row.
- Use at least 10 controlled reps each assistance set. Add 100–200 face pulls or band pull-aparts daily or at least every lifting day. No other assistance.

**Part 1, two cycles:** Four days/week, 10 jumps/throws, 5's PRO+5×5 FSL. Choose TM only after considering the whole workload; hold the same TM for both cycles. Keep assistance moderate in cycle 1 and push weight or reps in cycle 2. Finish lifting within 45 minutes without rushing; repeat Part 1 until capable.

**Part 2, two cycles:** Remove press and train squat/bench/deadlift three days/week. Begin with the same TM used in Part 1. Use 5's PRO+5×10; younger/less experienced lifters may use FSL, while experienced lifters may need a fixed load below FSL. Push assistance. Increase TM after each cycle. Keep the 45-minute standard; repeat this phase until capable.

**Part 3, one cycle:** Increase TM. Keep three days/week but use standard 5/3/1 PR sets + 5×5 FSL. Assistance and 45-minute limit remain.

On a later repeat, Part 1 may be omitted. An aggressive version performs both assistance movements after every main-lift set rather than alternating, but it is only for rare, highly prepared lifters who never lose technique. Adequate food is mandatory.

Conditioning is 2–3 easy/recovery days. Prepared athletes may run, but high running volume requires removing squat/deadlift 5×10 work. Athlete jumps/throws may gradually reach 20–30 per lifting day over months. BBS or SSL can eventually replace supplemental work, but the assistance exercise selection should stay fixed.

# Conditioning

## Match conditioning to the goal

Conditioning and lifting draw from the same recovery budget. Decide which quality has priority and make concessions elsewhere. Hard conditioning is performance-based work such as Prowler, sled, hills, stairs, and running. Easy conditioning is consistency-based aerobic work that should not impair lifting.

Easy conditioning is required even when hard conditioning is not. It improves between-session and between-set recovery, work capacity, resting heart rate, blood pressure, and general health. Build an aerobic base before harder work. Do not automatically spend improved fitness on more training; use it to perform the existing plan better.

For hard conditioning, use the “50% rule”: training must leave something in reserve relative to testing. This is a principle, not exact arithmetic. Make a known test easier by reducing load, increasing allowed time, or reducing work. Test during a 7th Week Protocol, not every session. If conditioning degrades the main goal or lifting degrades the conditioning goal, change the plan.

## Prowler

The Prowler is generally safe and low-impact, mostly concentric, usually produces little soreness, and is simple to use; the ankles and Achilles still require preparation and gradual adaptation. Surface dramatically changes difficulty, so prescriptions must be adjusted. Running or walking is valid; choose what the body tolerates.

A reference test is ten 40-yard pushes at bodyweight, including the Prowler, in 15 minutes. Heavier lifters may need a different goal. Training examples:

- 10×40 yards at 70–75% of goal load in 15 minutes.
- 10×40 at 100% of goal load in 20–22 minutes.
- 8×40 at 110–125% of goal load in 15 minutes.
- Infrequent untimed challenge: 2×40 at 70–75%, 2×40 at 100%, then 2×40 with the heaviest movable load.

Start light. Define a personal goal based on fitness and surface, train below test demand, and test in a 7th Week. Prowler work normally replaces single-leg assistance.

## Sled

Use a harness or hold straps; attaching a strap to a weight belt is inefficient and uncomfortable. Straps allow both forward and backward pulling. A reference test is ten 40-yard bodyweight pulls—five forward and five backward, alternating—in 15 minutes, with sled weight included. Keep the sled moving during difficult backward pulls. Use the same submaximal training principle as the Prowler. Sled conditioning normally replaces single-leg assistance.

## Hills and stairs

No two hills or staircases are identical. Set a site-specific performance goal, then train submaximally with the 50% rule. Hills stress ankles and Achilles; prepare them and allow gradual adaptation. At least two hill sessions/week are normally needed to improve. Stairs are often easier on the ankles. A 5–10%-bodyweight vest on stairs is only for people with a long history of conditioning, stairs/hills, and vest work.

## Running

Running is between jogging and sprinting—roughly 60–70% of maximum sprint speed. Sprinting is inappropriate for most unprepared lifters because calves and hamstrings are not adapted. Larger lifters should favor low-impact sled/bike work; lighter runners may tolerate more volume, but individual structure and response matter.

The basic goal is the ability to run a mile well for the individual. New or returning runners should use 1600 m total per workout, three times/week for one cycle, broken into 100–400 m repeats (for example 2×400+4×200, 4×400, 16×100, or 8×200). Rest as needed for 1–2 cycles. After adaptation, use roughly 3:1 rest-to-run time, optionally progressing to 2:1.

New runners should reduce lifting stress for at least two cycles: original 5/3/1 or 5's PRO+3–5×5 FSL, 0–50 assistance reps/category, no single-leg/lower-back assistance, and 10 jumps/throws. Running initially affects strength more than most conditioning.

Progress total per session from 1600 to 2400 then 3200 m while keeping repeats at 100–400 m. A useful Leader/Anchor organization holds 1600 m in Leaders and increases volume in Anchors. High squat volume and aggressively improving running rarely coexist well. Test the mile during the 7th Week rather than before adaptation.

## Easy conditioning

Easy work must not negatively affect lifting. Useful modes include bike/AirDyne, walking, vest walking, swimming, and easy cardio equipment; each still requires adaptation. It is especially valuable for older/experienced lifters, beginners, people carrying excess body fat, strength specialists, people with heart/blood-pressure risk, and anyone who struggles to finish lifting sessions.

Default guideline: three sessions of 30–45 minutes weekly. Bike or vest walking is generally least stressful. Keep aerobic work aerobic; hard-training personalities may need a heart-rate monitor to avoid racing it.

# Recovery

Recovery rests on four foundations: appropriate training, sustainable diet, sleep, and movement. Supplements and recovery gadgets come after these.

## Training and load management

Training must match present fitness, goals, life stress, and actual commitment. Outside stress still counts. Chronic soreness, fatigue, worsening performance, poor sleep, elevated resting heart rate, exhaustion, or impaired mental clarity call for adjustment; one bad workout or week does not.

Change one variable at a time and monitor it: exercise choice, lifting frequency, supplemental volume, assistance, conditioning, or recovery work. Do not stack BBB, FSL, PR sets, Jokers, maximal assistance, and hard Prowler work simply because each works alone. Long-term consistency with a modest coherent plan beats short bursts of unsustainable work.

## Diet and hydration

Diet must become a sustainable habit and support the current goal. Record simple cycle-specific targets and begin with habits the lifter can actually maintain.

Build meals around protein, useful carbohydrate, and fruit/vegetables; dietary fat commonly comes with whole-food protein or can be added. A simple starting meal uses roughly two fist-sized protein portions, two palm-sized carbohydrate portions, and two fist-sized fruit/vegetable portions. Adjust according to performance and body-composition direction:

- To emphasize protein/produce: three protein, one carbohydrate, three produce portions.
- To emphasize carbohydrate: one protein, three carbohydrate, two produce portions.
- To gain: increase meal count and/or protein/carbohydrate portions; liquid calories may help.
- To lose fat: reduce portions or carbohydrate while maintaining/increasing protein. Use gradual habits rather than crash diets.

Whole-food protein is preferred; roughly 1 g/lb bodyweight is presented as a common starting idea, but the text explicitly notes that many need less and individual workload matters. Start with a substantial protein portion each meal and adjust by recovery/performance. Choose fruits and vegetables that are tolerated and consistently eaten. Choose practical carbohydrate sources such as rice, potatoes, oats, and pasta; carbohydrates support training and should be scaled, not reflexively eliminated.

Meal frequency is schedule-dependent. Four complete meals/day is a practical starting point, not a physiological law. A concrete example is about 50 g whole-food protein per meal across four meals, then fruit/vegetables, then roughly 50–100 g carbohydrate, but this is an example rather than a universal prescription.

Hydration is individual. Build the habit gradually and avoid large intake just before sleep. One practical example is a bottle at each of four meals plus two during training (about 100 oz/day); a gallon is only a broad convention, not mandatory.

Fat loss should be slow enough to preserve strength, muscle, and habits. Anchor progress to performance and conditioning goals rather than relying only on scale/caliper changes.

## Sleep

Use consistent sleep/wake windows. Eight hours/night is a general guideline, adjusted for the individual and stressful life phases. Reduce late digital stimulation by scheduling email/phone use, write tomorrow's tasks down so they do not remain mentally active, complete important tasks during the day, and use relaxing reading before bed.

## Movement and active recovery

Active recovery is particularly useful for older lifters and competitive athletes but must never impair main training. Use it on non-lifting days, before hard off-day conditioning, or as brief morning/evening sessions. It can be as simple as 250–500 rope jumps plus Agile 8.

Example circuits, performed easily for 3–5 rounds:

1. AirDyne 3 minutes; hurdle duck-unders 5–10/side; leg kicks 10/leg; ab wheel 10; Spidermans 10/leg.
2. Rope 100; Turkish get-up 5/side; push-ups 10; hurdle duck-unders 5–10/side.
3. Jog 30–50 yards; Spidermans 10/leg; backward jog 30–50 yards; Turkish get-up 5/side; jog 30–50 yards; push-ups 10.

For limited space, slowly descend to and hold a squat 5–10 seconds; walk hands to push-up position; hold one Spiderman each side 5–10 seconds; perform 1–3 push-ups with a 5–10 second bottom hold; inch-worm back to standing. Repeat 10–20 times.

### Older athlete

An older athlete with long training/sport mileage may combine easy conditioning and mobility for about an hour on off days, provided intensity stays genuinely restorative. Use a 10–20%-bodyweight vest walk or moderate AirDyne; periodically stop for slow squats, Spidermans, Cossack squats, hip-flexor stretches, inchworms, and arm/neck circles.

A vest example is 3–4 miles with 3–4 mobility stops. A bike example is 10–15 minutes riding, then 2–3 mobility drills, repeated. The purpose is to break up the stiffness of one repeated movement pattern. Never require recovery from the recovery session.
